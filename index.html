Goals Gone Wild

What happens when good goals go bad?

Goal setting is the most prescribed tool in personal development. Specific, challenging goals have been shown to boost performance across hundreds of studies.

But what if the prescription has side effects?

You are about to meet three students starting university — just like you. Each one sets a goal. Your job: decide what they should do.

Alex has just arrived at university. Their parents are proud. Their school teachers said they had potential. Alex wants to prove them all right.

In their first coaching meeting, the coach asks: “What does success look like for you this year?”

Alex thinks for a moment and says: “I want to do really well academically. That’s the priority.”

The coach asks Alex to turn this into a clear goal.

What goal should Alex set?

Teacher: ask for a show of hands, then click the majority choice.

What actually happened

A. The 3.8 GPA — Alex hit 3.82. But by December, they had skipped every social event, dropped out of the sports club they’d joined in Week 1, and turned down a team consulting project that didn’t count toward their grade. In January, they felt isolated, couldn’t explain what skills they’d actually built, and had nothing to talk about in interviews except grades.

B. Three hours every evening — Alex sat at their desk reliably. But “study” became passive re-reading. The goal measured time, not learning. By November, Alex was logging hours but retaining little — and had stopped exercising, seeing friends, or sleeping properly to protect the routine.

C. “Do my best” — Without a clear target, Alex drifted. Some weeks they worked hard; others they didn’t. By mid-semester they couldn’t tell whether they were on track and felt anxious but directionless.

The pattern: Every option failed — but not because Alex chose wrong. The problem was upstream. Alex never defined what “success at university” actually meant beyond one dimension. A single metric replaced a complex aspiration, and everything not measured was abandoned.

To the room:

What did Alex actually want? Was it really just a number?

What got sacrificed — and why didn’t the goal protect it?

Sam played a lot of sport at school and wants to stay active at university. In their coaching meeting, Sam says: “I want to get properly fit — not just dabble.”

Together with their coach, Sam sets a goal: Run a half marathon before the end of the academic year.

By week 6, Sam can run 5K comfortably. They feel good. Then life gets busy — exams, group projects, a part-time job.

What should Sam do now?

Teacher: ask for a show of hands, then click the majority choice.

What actually happened

A. Push through — Sam forced early morning runs on too little sleep, skipped group project meetings to train, and picked up a knee injury in week 10. After two weeks off, they never went back. The goal that was supposed to build fitness destroyed it.

B. Drop it — Sam felt relieved for a week, then guilty. They’d “failed.” The experience of not finishing what they started eroded their confidence. When a friend suggested joining a climbing club, Sam thought: “What’s the point? I’ll just quit that too.”

C. Harder short-term target — The 22-minute goal added pressure to something that had been enjoyable. Runs became tests. Sam started skipping sessions they might “fail” and only ran on days they felt fast. By December they were running once a fortnight.

The pattern: Sam’s goal had no structure between “I want to be fit” and a specific race distance. When the aggressive target collided with real life, there was nothing to fall back on. Hitting it meant overreach; missing it meant failure; and achieving the smaller milestone (5K) felt like a ceiling, not a foundation.

To the room:

Sam could run 5K — that’s a real achievement. Why didn’t it feel like one?

What was missing between “I want to be fit” and “run a half marathon”?

Jordan is a talented photographer. They’ve done it since they were 14 — it’s the thing that makes them feel most like themselves. At university, Jordan sees classmates starting side projects and thinks: “I should make this count for something.”

In their coaching meeting, Jordan sets a goal: Earn £500 from photography by the end of semester one.

By week 9, Jordan has earned £310. Three weeks remain. The goal is slipping.

What should Jordan do?

Teacher: ask for a show of hands, then click the majority choice.

What actually happened

A. Grind to £500 — Jordan took three corporate jobs in two weeks. The work was tedious. They delivered acceptable but uninspired photos and started cutting corners on editing to meet deadlines. They hit £510 — and didn’t pick up a camera for pleasure for the next two months. The thing they loved became the thing they resented.

B. Lower the target — Jordan hit £380 and technically “succeeded.” But it felt hollow. The adjusted goal felt like an admission of inadequacy, and the entire semester’s photography was now framed by a number rather than by growth, creativity, or identity.

C. Forget the number — Jordan felt guilty about abandoning a goal. In their next coaching meeting, they couldn’t articulate what they’d achieved. Without any structure, “shoot what I love” produced great photos but no development direction, and Jordan struggled to explain their progress.

The pattern: Jordan attached a financial metric to something driven by identity and love of the craft. The extrinsic target didn’t support the intrinsic motivation — it replaced it. When the number came under pressure, Jordan faced a choice between compromising their standards or compromising their goal. No version of the £500 target could solve this, because the target itself was the problem.

To the room:

Jordan cares about photography. That didn’t change. So why did every option feel wrong?

Is the issue the specific number — or is it where the goal was aimed?

Three students. Nine options. Zero good outcomes.

Every goal was specific. Most were challenging. Several were measurable and time-bound. By every conventional standard, these were “good” goals.

Yet each one produced the same kinds of failure: tunnel vision, collapsed motivation, abandoned progress, eroded confidence, compromised standards.

The problem was never the goal itself. It was what was missing around it.

● Alex measured one thing and lost everything else.

▲ Sam had a destination but no route — and no reason to keep walking when the destination moved.

+ Jordan pointed a performance target at an identity — and the identity buckled.
